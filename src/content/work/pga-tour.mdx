## The problem

The PGA TOUR runs multiple tournaments simultaneously — often in different parts of the US and internationally — each with its own scoring cadence, branding, and fan base. Golf is a statistics-heavy sport, and scores operate on two timescales at once: within a single weekend of play and aggregated across an entire tournament season.

The app's existing content display had been built as monolithic, event-agnostic views. They couldn't flex to fit the context — there was no meaningful difference in how the app presented a tournament that was three days out versus one mid-Round 2. And because each tournament has its own brand identity, any visual customization had been bespoke work rather than a system.

Fans were also underserved on the features that sustain engagement during live events: tracking specific players, seeing the latest media, and moving quickly between leaderboard, tee times, and field views.

## My approach to the problem

The first question I brought to the project wasn't "what screens need to be redesigned" — it was "what's the smallest unit of data a golf fan actually cares about, and how do we design containers flexible enough to surface it in any context." Reframing the problem that way made the solution obvious: a component library, not a template refresh. The goal became a home screen composable from interchangeable pieces — configured for an upcoming tournament, a live Round 2, or a final leaderboard — and skinnable to match the branding of whichever event was active.

## Design approach

### Component architecture

Every piece of existing content was treated as a candidate for decomposition. The inventory question wasn't "what screens do we have" but "what are the smallest meaningful units of data a fan might care about" — and then designing containers for those units that could compose into any configuration needed.

The resulting library covered leaderboard rows, player cards, video highlight modules, score tickers, favorites tracking, tee time lists, and FedExCup standings — each designed to work standalone or in combination, and each inheriting tournament identity through a theming layer rather than one-off styling.

High-fidelity wireframes were produced for each component before visual design began, so the development team could start building component infrastructure in parallel. That sequencing compressed the overall build timeline.

### Tournament theming

Each PGA TOUR event has a distinct logo and color palette. Rather than treating that as a design problem to solve tournament by tournament, the component system was built with brand inheritance as a first-class feature: drop in a tournament's primary color and mark, and every component on the screen reflects it — the Quicken Loans National looks different from the Shaw Charity Classic without any additional design work per event.

### Home screen composition

The home screen shifted from a fixed layout to a slot-based system. For an upcoming tournament: featured players, tee times, and preview content. During live play: live leaderboard, video highlights, favorites tracking, and round-in-progress indicators. After a tournament closes: final standings, FedExCup implications, and recap content. Same components, different configuration.

## Outcome

The component library gave the PGA TOUR a durable asset rather than a one-time redesign. Every tournament going forward benefits from the same system — data surfaces cleanly whether the event is a major or a mid-season Champions Tour stop — and the branding adapts without bespoke design work each time.

Delivering high-fidelity wireframes before visual design was finalized allowed development to begin building component infrastructure early, shortening the overall build cycle.

The favorites tracking and media integration gave fans the engagement hooks that matter most during live rounds: following specific players through a day of play and staying current with video highlights without leaving the leaderboard context.

## What I'd do differently

The component system solved the per-tournament branding problem cleanly, but theming was still a manual designer handoff ahead of each event. With the volume of tournaments the TOUR runs, that's real overhead. I'd push to design the theming layer as a self-service tool for the content team from day one — so tournament setup requires no designer involvement at all.

The more strategic thing I'd do differently: make the system's long-term value visible to stakeholders earlier. A component library that serves 40+ tournaments is a significant organizational asset, but it looks like a design deliverable until someone does the math on what it displaces. I'd build that business case into the project framing from the start — because "we won't have to do bespoke design work for every event going forward" is a budget argument, not just a design one, and it's the argument that funds the next iteration of the system.
