## The problem

ON.Notate was built for a narrow, highly specialized user population — clinical professionals with deep oncology expertise who interact with the product all day, every day, in a high-stakes data environment. Standard UAT cycles at release time were happening, but they weren't generating the kind of comprehensive feedback needed to understand how the product was actually working for these users, where it was falling short, and what the roadmap should prioritize next.

The goal of the pilot research program was to fill that gap — not just to collect impressions, but to build a longitudinal picture of the product's usability and identify patterns that could drive concrete product decisions.

## Program design

I designed the research as a mixed-methods program: a quantitative survey completed by each participant, followed by a one-hour one-on-one qualitative interview. That pairing was intentional — the scores tell you *what* is happening to satisfaction and usability over time; the interviews tell you *why*.

The quantitative instrument included UMUX-Lite for standardized usability scoring and a net-promoter-style satisfaction measure. Both were chosen for their ability to track change across time, so the six-month gap between rounds would produce a meaningful before-and-after comparison rather than a snapshot.

The qualitative interview guide covered:

* Prior abstraction experience and tools used before ON.Notate
* Prior clinical experience and domain background
* A walkthrough of their typical workflow within ON.Notate — how they enter a chart, what information they orient to first, the mental model they use to establish patient context before working through documents
* Things they found missing, confusing, or harder than expected
* Things that worked better than comparable tools they'd used
* Questions they find themselves asking managers or colleagues — a reliable proxy for where the interface fails to communicate clearly
* Training and documentation needs

I ran all interviews myself — approximately twelve participants per round, each session an hour, facilitated one-on-one. Having a single interviewer across all sessions kept the data consistent and allowed me to build rapport with participants over time, which made the second-round conversations richer. Returning participants knew they could speak candidly about what hadn't improved.

## Analysis

Transcripts were analyzed using AI-assisted tools to surface patterns across sessions. I looked for:

* Themes that recurred across the majority of participants — high-confidence signals for prioritization
* One-off observations that might reflect individual background or experience differences rather than universal usability issues
* Challenges within the current product versus opportunities for improvement — separating "fix this" from "build this next"
* The relationship between quantitative scores and qualitative responses — what was driving a score up or down was almost always visible in the interview transcripts

The synthesis distinguished between low-hanging-fruit improvements (quick wins within the existing feature set) and higher-investment opportunities (new functionality or architectural changes warranting roadmap consideration).

## What the longitudinal structure made possible

Returning to the same participants six months later — after significant development had happened — changed the nature of the research considerably. Rather than asking users to evaluate a product cold, we could ask them specifically what they had noticed change, what still felt unresolved, and how their confidence with the tool had evolved. Participants who had flagged something as a pain point in round one could speak directly to whether it had been addressed.

The score comparison across rounds provided a concrete signal of overall direction — whether the product was moving toward or away from user expectations as it matured — and the qualitative layer explained what was driving that movement.

## Delivering the findings

For each round, I produced a prioritized findings report and delivered it as a staged readout to four distinct audiences — sequenced deliberately so that each group could engage with the findings in the context most relevant to them.

**UX team first.** The internal design team heard the findings before anyone else — both for the product-specific insights and as a model for how research could be structured and synthesized. This was as much a knowledge-sharing moment as a findings delivery.

**Product management.** The PM team had close relationships with these users and had often delivered training to them directly. Hearing user feedback through a research lens — rather than through support requests or ad hoc conversations — gave them a more structured picture of where users were struggling and why.

**Engineering.** Engineers on the team rarely had direct contact with users. The readout was an opportunity to close that gap: hearing feedback in users' own words, understanding the reasoning behind design and product decisions, and building a mental model of the people they were building for. The goal was to shift their frame from technically-focused decision-making to user-grounded decision-making — making them better partners in the product process, not just implementers of specs.

**Senior stakeholders.** Leadership responsible for budgeting and business decisions heard the findings last. For that audience, the research served a different purpose: demonstrating that roadmap decisions were grounded in real user feedback, not internal assumptions. It made the case for continued investment in the product by showing that the team was listening systematically, not just building on instinct.

## Outcome

The research program produced a prioritized findings report delivered across the full organization — from the design team through to senior leadership — directly informing the post-MVP product roadmap and separating improvements that could ship quickly from those requiring larger investment.

The longitudinal design also established a research relationship with a cohort of specialist users that the team could return to — an asset that continued to inform ON.Notate development beyond the formal program.

## What I'd do differently

The two-round structure captured a meaningful before-and-after, but six months is a long gap for a product in active development. I'd advocate for a lighter-weight touchpoint at the midpoint — not a full research round, but a brief check-in with a subset of participants — to catch emerging issues earlier and validate that roadmap priorities were still aligned with user needs as the product evolved between formal rounds.

I'd also formalize the connection between research findings and UAT cycles more explicitly from the start. The pilot research and ongoing UAT were tracked separately, but there was natural signal overlap. Building a shared tracking system that linked qualitative themes from the program to specific test cases in UAT would have made both more useful and created a tighter feedback loop between research insights and release validation.
