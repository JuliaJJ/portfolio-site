## The problem

In oncology research, data is only as useful as its structure. Clinicians generate enormous volumes of patient information — but most of it lives as unstructured text: physician notes, faxed lab results, imaging reports arriving in inconsistent formats from different vendors. You can't aggregate it, anonymize it, or run analysis on it while it's in that form.

Chart abstraction is the process of fixing that. A trained clinical specialist, typically a nurse or nurse practitioner with deep oncology knowledge, reviews a patient's raw documentation and transcribes the relevant data into structured fields. That structured data can then be used to assess drug efficacy, determine clinical trial eligibility, and, in aggregate anonymized form, inform pharmaceutical research and development.

ON.Notate is the tool that makes that process possible. At its core: a workspace that puts a patient's documents and a structured data entry interface side by side, so abstractors can move through clinical records and capture what they find without switching between applications.

## What I came into

I joined the project in the planning stages, before any designs existed, before the product had a defined shape. Engineering had a framework concept for the data layer, but the visual interface and the workflows users would take through it were entirely undefined.

Over the next two-plus years, I was the designer of record: sole designer for most of the product's lifecycle, working directly with the engineering lead and product manager to define not just the interface but the scope and roadmap of the product itself.

## Understanding the users

ON.Notate serves three distinct user types, two of whom interact with the data most directly.

**Chart abstractors** are the primary users. They are not generalists; these are clinically trained specialists, often nurses or nurse practitioners, with specific oncology expertise. That expertise matters because the data they're handling is precise and consequential: chemotherapy regimen components, biomarker results that vary by cancer type, lab values that require domain knowledge to correctly identify and place. Getting it wrong has downstream effects on research validity and, in some cases, patient safety.

**QC reviewers** follow abstractors through each chart, verifying that abstracted data was correctly identified from a source document, checking for errors, and resolving discrepancies. Every data field carries a document source, the specific clinical document where the value was found, so QC can always trace what was entered back to its origin. An audit trail records the full history of each field: whether the value was imported from the EHR, added by an abstractor, or corrected by a QC reviewer.

**Project administrators** set up and manage studies, grouping patient charts into cohorts by cancer type or research objective,  and can assign specific charts to specialist abstractors when a chart requires domain expertise in a particular area.

We conducted multiple research studies with these users throughout the build: a pilot study covering a subset of domains, a separate study where abstractors verified data surfaced by an NLP system, and ongoing video interviews tracking how user impressions evolved as the product matured. The same users were engaged over time, giving us longitudinal visibility into how the product was landing.

## The hardest design problems

### State at every level

ON.Notate is a deeply stateful application. A chart has a status. A domain within a chart has a status. An individual data field has a status. A document has a review status. When multiple abstractors work the same chart simultaneously, their contributions have to be tracked and attributed individually. The same chart moves through roughly a dozen states, from abstraction in progress, to abstraction complete, to QC in progress and so on, and the interface has to surface the right information and actions for each persona at each stage.

Designing that state system, making it legible without overwhelming the interface, was one of the most sustained challenges of the project.

### A design system built for a different product

The existing Ontada design system was built for Ontada Health, a patient-facing product with a very different data model and interaction pattern than an internal clinical tool. ON.Notate needed to handle data types, input patterns, and display states the system was never designed for. Rather than abandoning the system, I worked with engineering to extend it, building custom components that coexisted with the existing system's API, matched its visual language, and could accommodate the specific demands of chart abstraction. Those components eventually fed back into a broader design system initiative.

### Conditional form logic across 24 domains

Each of the 24 domains has its own form, and many of those forms contain conditional logic: fields that appear or change based on diagnosis type, medication category, dosage, or other upstream values. Tracking those variants, designing for them consistently, and communicating them to engineering in a way that could be built without ambiguity was a significant coordination challenge across the full project arc.

### Inverting the requirements process

Business requirements for this product were frequently undefined or too abstract to build from directly. Rather than waiting for requirements to materialize, I designed ahead of them, producing interface specifications that product could then use as the basis for writing formal business and functional requirements. This inverted the typical design-follows-requirements flow and required close, ongoing collaboration with the product manager to make sure the designs were solving the right problems before they became the documentation of record.

## Workspace design

### Entry: project dashboard

Users arrive at a dashboard showing cards for each study they're assigned to: study name, patient count, and status. A single abstractor might be working across five or six studies simultaneously, each covering a different cancer type or research objective.

### Project view

Clicking into a study surfaces a status banner showing aggregate chart progress across the pipeline followed by the user's personal task queue. Abstractors can hold up to five charts in progress at once; QC reviewers can hold ten. Charts beyond that stay in a common queue and are retrieved on demand, preventing work hoarding. Admins can assign specific charts to specialist users when a domain requires particular clinical expertise.

### Patient chart workspace

The workspace is a three-panel layout:

**Chart documents.** A filterable list of all documents attached to the patient's chart, with date, author, and review status. Green indicates reviewed; orange indicates not yet reviewed. This panel can be collapsed on smaller screens to give more room to the center and right panels.

**Document viewer.** A document selected on the left loads here in full. Controls allow zooming, moving between documents, flagging documents that have been incorrectly attributed to the wrong patient, and marking documents as reviewed. This is the raw, unstructured clinical record they're working from.

**Abstraction space.** An accordion of all 24 domains. Each domain expands to show a timeline view, a table of all records entered for that domain, which may have one row or many depending on the domain data. Clicking a row opens the domain form. Every value entered in the form carries a document source linking it back to the clinical document where it was found. Below the form, a full audit history shows the provenance of every field and who made each change.

## Outcome

ON.Notate shipped as a production tool within Ontada's oncology data platform. The integrated document-and-form workspace, putting the source document and the abstraction form in the same screen, was consistently cited by users as a meaningful improvement over the prior workflow, which required running an EHR and a separate abstraction tool side by side.

The audit trail and document provenance system met data quality and compliance requirements for data used in pharmaceutical research and clinical trial eligibility, a non-negotiable bar for a tool operating in this space.

The design system extensions built for ON.Notate became the catalyst for a broader initiative, the Ontada AI-Ready Design System, specifically because the experience of building custom components exposed a structural problem: getting anything non-standard into the core Ontada design system required going through a central governance team, creating a timeline bottleneck that product teams couldn't afford. The AI-ready system was designed to solve that directly.

## What I'd do differently

About a year into the project, design-engineering misalignments had become a real problem: designs were drifting from what engineers considered technically feasible, and catching those gaps late was expensive to fix. The solution was structural: we formalized twice-weekly standing meetings with design, product, and engineering to continuously review requirements and in-progress work together. That tripod alignment resolved the problem, and the cadence held for the remainder of the project. If I were starting over, I'd establish that structure from day one rather than arriving at it a year in.

I'd also push harder at the outset to define the state model as a standalone design artifact — a map of every object in the system, its possible states, and the transitions between them — before any screen design began. We built that understanding incrementally over the course of the project, which meant some early designs had to be revisited when we discovered state interactions we hadn't anticipated. Starting with the state model would have made the entire build more coherent from the start.
