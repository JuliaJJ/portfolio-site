## The problem

Roughly 36% of U.S. adults have low health literacy — meaning they struggle to understand health information in written form. In hospitals, this creates compounding failures: patients who can't read signs miss appointments, end up in the wrong department, or can't tell staff where they're trying to go.

Most hospital wayfinding is designed by facilities teams optimizing for cost and code compliance — not by designers thinking about the patient who is scared, in pain, and trying to find radiology on the third floor of a building they've never entered before.

I wanted to explore what a wayfinding system would look like if we designed it from the failure mode up.

## Constraints I set for myself

1. **Zero reliance on written English.** Every element had to be legible to someone who cannot read.
2. **Works at distance and in motion.** Signs need to read from a wheelchair being pushed at a fast walk.
3. **Learnable in under five minutes.** First-time patients can't be expected to study a legend.
4. **Doesn't condescend.** Pictogram systems can feel infantilizing. Dignity matters.

## What I explored

I started by mapping the twelve most common patient destinations in a general acute hospital — ED, radiology, lab, pharmacy, surgical, oncology, ICU, maternity, outpatient clinics, chapel, cafeteria, main exit. Then I looked at every existing pictogram system I could find: ISO 7010, AIGA symbols, airport wayfinding, Tokyo subway design.

The core insight: most medical pictogram systems are designed for staff, not patients. They encode clinical categories — *imaging* vs. *laboratory* — rather than patient goals: *I need a blood draw.*

I rebuilt the destination set around patient intent:

- **Where I'm going** — appointments, procedures, scheduled visits
- **What I need** — food, toilet, pharmacy, a place to sit
- **Who I'm looking for** — nurse station, help point, my care team
- **Getting out** — exit, parking, transit

## The visual system

Color carries the load that text usually does. I used a four-color system with strong luminance contrast between each pair:

- **Deep teal** — clinical destinations (procedures, imaging, labs)
- **Amber** — support services (pharmacy, cafeteria, waiting areas)
- **Slate** — navigation (exits, elevators, parking)
- **White on red** — emergency and help

Shape reinforces color for colorblind accessibility: each category has a distinct geometric container (circle, square, shield, diamond). You can navigate the system using color alone or shape alone.

Icons were built on a grid with proportional human figures performing recognizable actions — not flat abstract glyphs. The difference is subtle but meaningful: a figure actively handing something to another figure reads as *pharmacy* more reliably than a stylized pill symbol does.

## What I learned

The hardest constraint was the no-condescension rule. Simplified pictograms often strip so much context they feel designed for children — and that's alienating to adult patients navigating a genuinely difficult situation. The fix was keeping figures proportional and actions specific. A person seated in a waiting chair with a number indicator reads as *take a number and wait* across cultures and reading levels.

I also found that the failure mode isn't usually the signs themselves — it's the *spacing* between them. Decision points (elevator banks, corridor intersections) need signage within five feet of the choice, not twenty. That's a facilities and installation problem, not a design problem. Part of the work is knowing where your leverage ends.

This stays a concept because deploying it would require facilities buy-in, code review, and a significant installation project across a live hospital. But the constraints it surfaced — intent-based information architecture, redundant encoding across color and shape, decision-point proximity — are transferable to any complex navigation problem.
